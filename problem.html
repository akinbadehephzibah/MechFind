Why we built Mech Find

The Problem

Who is affected · What it costs · Why nothing has been enough

Finding the wrong mechanic should not be a Nigerian rite of passage. We asked four questions and refused to stop until we had a serious answer to each one.

Our framework

Four questions. Honest answers.

This is the research that shaped the product. Figures are working estimates drawn from our interviews and desk research during the build.

01

Who is affected?

Every Nigerian driver of an imported or locally assembled car. The danfo driver whose Hiace breaks down mid-route. The lawyer whose Lexus develops an electrical fault. The trader whose Honda Accord alternator dies weeks after a “repair”.

That is roughly 22 million vehicles on Nigerian roads, against a vehicle-to-licensed-mechanic ratio we estimate at 180:1.

02

What does it cost them?

It costs them twice. Once for the wrong repair — typically ₦25,000 to ₦180,000 — and again for the correct one. On top of that they lose days of mobility: work, school runs, business deliveries.

Across the country, our research suggests Nigerian households absorb roughly ₦450 billion a year in avoidable repair costs caused by misdiagnosis, counterfeit parts and repeat failures.

03

What already exists?

Roadside mechanics. Town mechanics. Specialist workshops. Government-approved garages. Manufacturer service centres. WhatsApp groups full of “my guy” referrals. National automotive council listings.

They all exist. None of them connect a car owner to the right specialist for a specific fault, within a specific distance, inside a specific budget.

04

Why is it still not enough?

Because there is no trust signal beyond word of mouth. No place to see a mechanic’s verified skills, their past work, their distance from you and their availability — and then reach them in one tap.

That gap is the entire reason this platform exists.

Field research

The stories behind the numbers.

Six accounts gathered during our research week. Names and locations are as given to us by the car owners.

Stranded on Third Mainland Bridge

Bosede · Lekki to Victoria Island. Honda Accord, 2014. The alternator warning flickered, then the car died mid-bridge at 7:14am. Towing quoted at ₦60,000. Her regular mechanic did not return calls. Two hours in traffic and a lost ₦25,000 client meeting.

The counterfeit spark plug

Kabiru · Kano. Bought “original” plugs at ₦4,500 each from a roadside vendor. Two weeks later the misfiring was worse. A second mechanic showed him the plugs were counterfeit, sold at a 70% markup.

The AC compressor saga

Tunde · Abuja. Paid ₦150,000 for a “rebuilt” AC compressor. It failed after six weeks. The next mechanic quoted ₦210,000 for a genuine unit. Total loss: ₦150,000 and three weeks of commuting without air conditioning.

“My guy no dey answer”

Chinwe · Port Harcourt. A Maxima gearbox problem. Her “guy” referred her to another guy, who referred her to a third. Three weeks of phone tag and no repair at the end of it.

The roadside rescue gap

Ibrahim · Ibadan. Car died at 9pm. On-the-spot “mechanics” quoted ₦80,000 and above. None took card payment. None wrote a receipt. None had a shop address he could return to.

The repeat failure

Aisha · Abuja. Toyota Corolla. Paid ₦65,000 for what was diagnosed as a transmission fault. It turned out to be an ignition coil. The wrong diagnosis cost her the money and the time, twice over.

What we concluded

The problem is not finding a mechanic. It is finding the right one.

Mechanics are everywhere. Relevance, verification and price transparency are not. Every design decision on this platform follows from that single distinction.

Three things a car owner needs before contact

  • Relevance — does this mechanic work on my car and my fault?
  • Proximity — can they reach me, or I them, right now?
  • Trust — has anyone verified this person, and what did previous customers say?
